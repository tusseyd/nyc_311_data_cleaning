\begin{center}
{\Large\bfseries Response to the Referee}\\[0.5em]
{\large\itshape Principles for Open Data Curation:\\
A Case Study with the New York City 311 Service Request Data}
\end{center}

\bigskip

We thank the Editor and the Referee for their careful reading and
helpful comments, and we appreciate the Referee's positive assessment
of the paper. We have revised the manuscript in response; the main
changes are:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Figures.} Figures have been revised, replaced, or
  removed to improve readability, with larger fonts, explicit units,
  and expanded captions that state each figure's key observations.
  One figure has been replaced by a table, and figures and tables have
  been renumbered accordingly.
\item \textbf{Durations.} We added an explanation of why many long
  durations are plausible given the agency and complaint type, and we
  now summarize negative durations numerically in the text.
\item \textbf{Data curation roles.} Following the Referee's
  suggestion, the Collaboration step now recommends formalizing a data
  curation responsibility within each agency.
\item \textbf{Clarity and corrections.} We added examples of redundant
  fields, removed a paragraph on embedded commas after verifying that
  the export handles them correctly, and corrected the typographical
  issues the Referee identified.
\end{enumerate}

Point-by-point responses follow, with the Referee's comments in
italics. Page and line numbers refer to the revised manuscript. Where
a figure has been renumbered, we give both its original and revised
numbers. Tables in this letter are numbered R1 and R2 to distinguish
them from tables in the manuscript.

\refcomment{Page 4, lines 31--32: The authors mention two potentially
redundant pairs. This is discussed later, but it might help to add
something like ``e.g., cross\_street and intersection\_street'' at this
point in the article.}

We thank the Referee for this suggestion, which improves the clarity of
the paragraph. We now give the \texttt{cross\_street} and
\texttt{intersection\_street} pairs as examples at first mention
(p.~4, ll.~8--9), which now reads: ``The dataset contains seven
street-related fields, including two likely redundant pairs, e.g.,
\texttt{cross\_street\_1} \& \texttt{intersection\_\allowbreak street\_1}
and \texttt{cross\_street\_2} \& \texttt{intersection\_\allowbreak street\_2}.''

\refcomment{Page 4, bottom: This is just a comment for what it's worth.
I was doing text analysis on data like this that contained a Notes
field. It was a CSV file, and I imported it into MATLAB. I ended up
with a strange number of columns when importing it. It turns out that
the commas inside the Notes field were telling MATLAB that there were
multiple columns from the Notes. So, CSV can be dangerous or
problematic with free-form text fields. You are alluding to problems
like this at the bottom of the page. Just wanted to pass along my
experience.}

We thank the Referee for sharing this experience, which prompted us to
explore and verify how the NYC Open Data portal handles embedded commas. The
portal runs on the Socrata platform (now part of Tyler Technologies), a
mature and widely used infrastructure. Socrata's documentation states
that ``strings containing embedded commas, newlines, or quotes will
themselves be quoted,'' with embedded quotes escaped by doubling
\citep{socrata_csv}.

This behavior matches RFC~4180 \citep{rfc4180}, Rule~6: ``Fields
containing line breaks (CRLF), double quotes, and commas should be
enclosed in double-quotes.'' Although RFC~4180 is an informational memo
rather than a formal standard, it documents common practice and is the
closest thing to an official specification for the CSV format; it is
widely used as the reference for CSV parsers and validators. It was
published by the Internet Engineering Task Force (IETF), the body
responsible for core internet protocols and formats.

Our examination of the raw CSV file exported from the NYC Open Data
portal confirms this behavior. For example, the \texttt{location} field
contains a comma by design, separating latitude and longitude, and
appears in the raw file enclosed in double quotation marks:


\begin{center}
\texttt{"(40.64224331435751, -74.00707333743664)"}
\end{center}


In order to explore the Referee's concern further, we scanned the full,
unmodified export (approximately 16.0 million records; \citealp{nyc311_data}) to 
check its compliance with RFC~4180. We found embedded commas in 
15 of the 41 fields; Table~\ref{tab:embedded-commas} summarizes the results.

Besides \texttt{location}, which always contains a comma, over 2.6
million values contain commas, mostly in the free-text
\texttt{resolution\_description} field ($\approx$15\% of records), and
$\approx$163{,}000 values contain escaped double quotes. Nevertheless,
every record parses to the expected 41 columns with the
\texttt{fread()} function from the \texttt{data.table} package,
confirming that all embedded commas and quotes are correctly quoted and
escaped.

The Referee's experience is therefore highly relevant: a parser that
does not honor CSV quoting would misread a substantial share of these
records. Because the Socrata export quotes these fields correctly, and
quote-aware readers such as \texttt{fread()} \citep{datatable2025} interpret 
that quoting correctly, all fields are read as intended. 

We have therefore removed the paragraph on this 
issue (formerly p.~4, ll.~45--46), as the problem
it anticipated does not arise with these data.

\refcomment{Figure 3: First, this is very hard to read. The fonts are

We agree, and we thank the Referee for the suggestion to show only the

\refcomment{Page 13, positive durations: Do the long durations make
sense given the agency? If so, some words here might help readers.}

We thank the Referee for this question, which required some additional
analysis. We extracted the SRs with long (and extreme) durations and
examined their associated agencies and \texttt{complaint\_type} values.
Table~\ref{tab:long-durations}, at the end of this letter, summarizes
the results by agency, showing the average duration and count for each
complaint type.

Most of these long-duration SRs are associated with complaints that
could plausibly take a long time to resolve. For example, many of the
Department of Parks and Recreation (DPR) SRs concern trees (new tree
requests and overgrown, damaged, or dead trees), which can require
lengthy efforts to plant a new tree or remove a dead or dying one.
Likewise, the Department of Buildings (DOB) handles SRs involving
plumbing, building use, and construction, which may also take
considerable time to resolve (p.~13, l.~4--8).

Accordingly, we have revised the manuscript to include similar examples
and to note that such SRs can reasonably require long resolution times.

\refcomment{Page 17, Actionable Steps: I really liked these. I have one
suggestion (take it or leave it) on Collaboration. Agencies could
(should?) appoint a Data Curation Officer.}

We thank the Referee for the kind words and for this suggestion,
which we have adopted. NYC's Open Data Law, Local Law~11 of 2012
\citep{nyc_ll11}, already requires each agency to designate an Open
Data Coordinator. However, the Coordinator's formal responsibilities,
as set out in the City's Open Data Technical Standards Manual
\citep{nyc_tsm}, focus on publishing, compliance reporting, and public
outreach; none addresses data quality or curation. In our experience,
the role is also typically assigned in addition to staff members' core
duties, with limited training and frequent turnover.

We have expanded the Collaboration step (p.~16, l.~46 to p.~17, l.~12) to describe
this existing structure and to recommend formalizing a data curation
responsibility, either by extending the Coordinator role or by
appointing a dedicated Data Curation Officer, as the Referee suggests.
